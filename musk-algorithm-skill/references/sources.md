## 1. Canonical written — Isaacson 2023

Setting (Isaacson): at Tesla/SpaceX production meetings there is a
“nontrivial chance” Musk will intone, “like a mantra, what he calls
‘the algorithm.’” Shaped by production-hell surges at Nevada and
Fremont.

Musk (Isaacson): “I became a broken record on the algorithm… But I
think it’s helpful to say it to an annoying degree.”

Isaacson: “It had five commandments:”

### The five commandments (Isaacson quoting Musk)

**1. Question every requirement.** “Each should come with the name of
the person who made it. You should never accept that a requirement came
from a department, such as from ‘the legal department’ or ‘the safety
department.’ You need to know the name of the real person who made that
requirement. Then you should question it, no matter how smart that
person is. Requirements from smart people are the most dangerous,
because people are less likely to question them. Always do so, even if
the requirement came from me. Then make the requirements less dumb.”

**2. Delete any part or process you can.** “You may have to add them
back later. In fact, if you do not end up adding back at least 10% of
them, then you didn’t delete enough.”

**3. Simplify and optimize.** “This should come after step two. A
common mistake is to simplify and optimize a part or a process that
should not exist.”

**4. Accelerate cycle time.** “Every process can be speeded up. But
only do this after you have followed the first three steps. In the
Tesla factory, I mistakenly spent a lot of time accelerating processes
that I later realized should have been deleted.”

**5. Automate.** “That comes last. The big mistake in Nevada and at
Fremont was that I began by trying to automate every step. We should
have waited until all the requirements had been questioned, parts and
processes deleted, and the bugs were shaken out.”

### Corollaries (Isaacson; same passage; not the five steps)

- Hands-on technical managers (software managers ≥20% coding; solar-roof
  managers on the roof). “Otherwise, they are like a cavalry leader who
  can’t ride a horse or a general who can’t use a sword.”
- “Comradery is dangerous.” (Isaacson spelling in several excerpts.)
- “It’s OK to be wrong. Just don’t be confident and wrong.”
- “Never ask your troops to do something you’re not willing to do.”
- Skip-level meetings when solving problems.
- Hire for attitude; “Skills can be taught. Attitude changes require a
  brain transplant.”
- “A maniacal sense of urgency is our operating principle.”
- “The only rules are the ones dictated by the laws of physics.
  Everything else is a recommendation.”

### Origin of step 1 (Isaacson)

Rocket parts had “hundreds of specifications and requirements mandated
by the military and NASA.” Musk made engineers question all specs.
“This would later become step one in a five-point checklist, dubbed
‘the algorithm.’” “The military” or “the legal department” was not
enough — name the actual person. Tim Buzza (then SpaceX VP): they would
cite a military spec; Musk: “Who wrote that? Why does it make sense?”

### 10% add-back illustration (Isaacson; solar-roof nails)

Tesla solar-roof install: Musk ordered one nail instead of two per
foot; tiles popped; the team restored the second nail. Isaacson: “It
was part of the algorithm: if you don’t end up having to restore 10
percent of the parts you deleted, then you didn’t delete enough.”

### Nevada / Fremont / automate-first

Isaacson locates the algorithm in Model 3 “production hell” at
Gigafactory Nevada and Fremont. Musk tried to automate every step first
(“alien dreadnought”). Primary Musk, 13 Apr 2018 (X): “Yes, excessive
automation at Tesla was a mistake. To be precise, my mistake. Humans
are underrated.”

**Uncertain:** exact hardcover vs Kindle page split (Independent:
284–6 hardback; other recaps Kindle 284–285). Use ~pp. 284–286.

## 2. Canonical spoken origin — Everyday Astronaut, Aug 2021

Must be done **in order**. Close recaps: Everyday Astronaut page;
[Entrepreneur 2021-08-12](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/elon-musks-design-process-starts-with-making-things-less/380078)
(Trung Phan transcript, edited).

**1. Make the requirements less dumb.** “Your requirements are
definitely dumb. It does not matter who gave them to you. It’s
particularly dangerous if a smart person gave you the requirements,
because you might not question them enough.” “Everyone’s wrong. No
matter who you are, everyone is wrong some of the time.” “All designs
are wrong, it’s just a matter of how wrong.”

Named owner (spoken): “Whatever requirement or constraint you have it
must come with a name, not a department, because you can’t ask the
department, you have to ask a person.” Intern-who-left: a requirement
an intern “randomly came up with off the cuff” who is “not even at the
company anymore.”

**2. Try very hard to delete the part or process.** Bias toward “let’s
add this part or process step in case we need it.” “If you’re not
adding things back in at least 10% of the time, you’re clearly not
deleting enough.”

**3. Simplify or optimize — third, not first.** “Possibly the most
common error of a smart engineer is to optimize a thing that should not
exist.”

**4. Accelerate cycle time.** “You’re moving too slowly, go faster!
But don’t go faster until you’ve worked on the other three things
first.” “If you’re digging your grave, you don’t want to dig faster.
You want to stop digging your grave.”

**5. Automate last.** Musk: “I have personally made the mistake of
going backwards on all five steps multiple times” — Model 3: automate,
then simplify, then delete.

**Model 3 fiberglass mats** (best-sourced unowned/confused-requirement
story): mats between floor pan and battery pack. Teams disagreed (NVH
vs fire). A/B with a microphone: no audible difference — **deleted the
part**, bypassing ~$2M of robotics. Reverse-order: fix the robot, then
accelerate, then optimize glue, then ask what the mats were for.
Supporting: Tesla Q1 2018 earnings — automating placement of the
fiberglass mats (“basically fluff”) was “ironically foolish.”

## 3. Canonical mantra — Lex Fridman #438, Aug 2024

Official transcript, section “Elon’s approach to problem-solving,”
~00:43:47–00:48:36.

Musk (~00:44:08): “I have this very basic first principles algorithm
that I run kind of as a mantra, which is to first question the
requirements, make the requirements less dumb. The requirements are
always dumb to some degree… no matter how smart the person is who gave
you those requirements… You have to start there, because otherwise you
could get the perfect answer to the wrong question.”

Delete: “If you’re not forced to put back at least 10% of what you
delete, you’re not deleting enough.” Never putting anything back is
“overly conservative.” “The most common mistake of smart engineers is
to optimize a thing that should not exist.”

Psychology of delete: people remember with “jarring” pain the one time
they deleted something needed, then overcorrect. “We’re deliberately
going to delete more than we should.” “Cortical override to a limbic
instinct.” Warn the team in advance that some deletions will be put
back.

Step 4: “any given thing can be sped up… But you shouldn’t speed things
up until you’ve tried to delete it and optimize… Speeding up something
that shouldn’t exist is absurd.”

Step 5 + gone-backwards (skill-canonical for order): “And then, the
fifth thing is to automate it. I’ve gone backwards so many times where
I’ve automated something, sped it up, simplified it, and then deleted
it. And I got tired of doing that. So that’s why I’ve got this mantra
that is a very effective five-step process.”

Lex: deleting after you’ve already automated “must be real painful.”
Musk: “Wow, I really wasted a lot of effort there.”

Use Lex for: “first principles algorithm,” “mantra,”
perfect-answer-to-wrong-question, 10% put-back psychology,
gone-backwards sequence (automate → speed up → simplify → delete).

## 4. Why order matters

Same argument in all three sources.

| If you skip ahead | Failure mode | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Simplify/optimize first | “Optimize a thing that should not exist” | Starbase 2021; Isaacson step 3; Lex 2024 |
| Accelerate before 1–3 | Tesla: accelerating processes that should have been deleted; grave-digging | Isaacson step 4; Starbase 2021 |
| Automate first | Nevada + Fremont; Model 3 reverse-order; 2018 “excessive automation… my mistake” | Isaacson step 5; 2018 tweet; Starbase/Lex |
| Never add back 10% | You did not delete enough; over-conservative leftover waste | All three |

Sequence is the algorithm. Automating or speeding a step that should
not exist multiplies waste.
